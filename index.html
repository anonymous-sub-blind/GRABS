Abstract

Demand-driven navigation (DDN) asks a robot to satisfy an implicit request such as “I am thirsty” with any suitable object, rather than the named target of object goal navigation. Yet water, juice, and coffee satisfy it unequally, so the first acceptable object may not be the best. We formulate best-available demand-driven navigation: retrieving the highest-satisfaction object present while balancing quality against time, generalizing both. We present GRABS, a belief-guided framework that needs only a room-level floor plan. A large language model grounds the request into candidate categories with graded satisfaction probabilities, and a second query estimates each candidate's likelihood per room; these define a room utility, the expected satisfaction from searching each room, that a time-aware Markov decision process (MDP) balances against travel and search time. A vision-language model (VLM) verifies candidates, and an unsuccessful search lowers that room's belief before replanning. Holding an acceptable but suboptimal object, the robot asks whether to continue only when finding a better one would exceed a patience threshold. In AI Habitat, Isaac Sim, and hardware trials, GRABS matches the best baselines on explicit requests, raises absolute best-object accuracy by 27.8%, 29.6%, and 27.1% on HM3D, MP3D, and Isaac Sim over a first-acceptable-object baseline, and its time-aware MDP reaches them in up to 29.9% less time than heuristic planning. On hardware, it retrieves the best object for every vague request and asks the user when required.

Best-Available Demand-Driven Navigation

Existing demand-driven navigation treats satisfaction as binary: an object either satisfies the request or it does not, so a cup of coffee and a bottle of water count as equally good answers to “I am thirsty.” None of these methods chooses among substitutable candidates that satisfy the request to different degrees, and none decides whether continuing to search is worth the user's time.

We instead assign each candidate a graded satisfaction probability and ask the robot to retrieve the highest-satisfaction object that is actually present, while balancing quality against search time and the user's willingness to wait. The formulation contains both prior tasks as special cases:

  • Demand-driven navigation is the case with binary satisfaction and several acceptable categories.
  • Explicit object goal navigation restricts it further to a single named category.
The robot receives only a request and a room-level floor plan with semantic room labels. Object locations, states, and the set of objects present are unknown and must be inferred and verified during the search.

Method Overview

1

Intent grounding

An LLM turns the request into candidate categories, each with a satisfaction probability, plus a verification criterion (attributes, relations, or states the object must meet).

2

Spatial grounding

A second LLM query estimates how likely each candidate is to occur in each labeled room, using only the room labels of the floor plan. Together these define a room utility: the expected satisfaction gained by searching that room.

3

Time-aware MDP planning

Room selection is a small MDP over rooms with an absorbing goal state. Searching a room succeeds with probability equal to its utility, and the cost is travel time plus a fixed inspection time. Value iteration yields the next room; the plan is recomputed after every observation.

4

Verification and belief update

A VLM detects candidates in the 360° observation and checks the verification criterion. After a miss, the room's belief for each unverified candidate is lowered by a recall-aware Bayesian update, and candidates that cannot improve on the object in hand are dropped.

5

Time-aware interaction

Holding an acceptable but suboptimal object, the robot compares the expected time to find a better one with a patience threshold. Only when that time exceeds the threshold does it ask the user whether to continue.

Baselines

  • Simple planner — our MDP ablation. Same graded grounding and beliefs, but the next room is chosen by a heuristic utility-to-distance ratio instead of solving the MDP.
  • No-intent planner — scores rooms directly from the request without graded candidates and stops at the first object that satisfies the request, as most existing demand-driven methods do.
  • Direct GPT planner — an LLM generates a fixed room order once, with no spatial beliefs or replanning.
  • Landmark planner — grounds the request to a single landmark object instead of a graded candidate list.
  • Random planner — visits rooms in a seeded random order, the room-level analogue of target-agnostic frontier exploration.

Starred variants (★) add an LLM judge that decides whether to keep searching after a satisfactory object is found; they apply to the vague-intent columns only.

Key findings

  • Explicit requests are not compromised. GRABS reaches 96.5% SR and 80.0% SPL on HM3D, within 0.4% and 0.3% of the highest results, ties the highest SR on MP3D at 97.7% with the highest SPL at 88.3%, and has the highest SR and SPL in Isaac Sim.
  • Graded intent grounding drives the vague-intent gains. Best-object accuracy improves by 27.8% (HM3D), 29.6% (MP3D), and 27.1% (Isaac Sim) absolute over the No-intent planner, which stops at the first acceptable object, and by 11.5% and 8.0% over the Landmark planner on HM3D and MP3D.
  • The time-aware MDP improves the quality-time trade-off. Against the Simple planner, which shares the same grounding and beliefs, GRABS achieves higher Best, Top-2, and mean satisfaction while cutting search time from 140.3 to 98.4 s on HM3D and from 122.7 to 86.0 s on MP3D (29.9% less), and from 166.0 to 120.8 s in Isaac Sim.
  • Speed alone is not success. The No-intent and Direct GPT planners finish faster, but their lower Best and mean satisfaction show that this comes from accepting lower-quality candidates sooner.

Hardware Trials

The hardware trials run on a quadruped robot with a 360° camera payload in the physical office that the Isaac Sim environment replicates. The office is left in its existing state. GRABS retrieves the best available object in all four vague-intent trials, with a mean completion time of 182.8 s, and completes the explicit relational request with an SPL of 32.8%.

Final observations of the five trials

Vague intent · Human need “I am very thirsty.”

Retrieved: bottled water (satisfaction 1.0) in the dining area after 6 room visits, 320.2 s. Tea and coffee were found on the way; the robot asked the user once and then continued on its own.

Vague intent · Affordance “I need something to watch a presentation with a group.”

Retrieved: television (satisfaction 0.8) in the conference room after 1 room visit, 93.4 s. The robot asked whether to keep searching for a higher-ranked display; the user declined, so it delivered the television.

Vague intent · Situational “I spilled some coffee on the table.”

Retrieved: paper towel (satisfaction 1.0) in the kitchen after 3 room visits, 146.9 s.

Vague intent · Preference “Which seat would be most comfortable for resting?”

Retrieved: sofa (satisfaction 1.0) in the reception area after 3 room visits, 170.6 s.

Explicit · Relational “Find a silver trash bin near a door.”

Retrieved: the silver trash bin beside a door in the office area after 4 room visits, 71.8 m and 263.5 s (SPL 32.8%). A silver bin away from any door and a black bin were rejected, so category, attribute, and spatial relation were verified jointly.

Limitations and Future Work

GRABS assumes a floor plan with semantically labeled rooms and reasons at room granularity, so it does not address search within a room. Satisfaction probabilities and the patience threshold are fixed and do not adapt to individual users. Future work includes learning satisfaction values and a patience model adapted per user from interaction feedback, and combining room-level beliefs with frontier-based exploration when no floor plan is available.
